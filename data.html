World events: how the headlines are chosen

Official statements come from the UK Foreign Office and the European Commission feeds. They show the title, the party, the time, and a link.

Outlet headlines come from the GDELT Project. We read its public 15 minute files for the last hour. We keep English pages that GDELT tags with one of these themes: armed conflict, election, protest, terror, legislation, national security, or general government. Headlines about the same event are grouped by similar wording. A group needs at least 2 different sites. We show at most 3 sites. We rank a group by the number of sites.

Local newspaper groups often repost one story, so a count can overstate independent coverage. The theme tags and the grouping are automatic and can be wrong. We do not check the claims.

Context on cards

For some items the card shows a short Wikipedia summary, a Wikimedia Commons photo, and a NASA satellite view. We match a Wikipedia page only by exact title and only for volcanoes, US states and countries, and we skip a page that does not look like the right kind of place. Wikipedia text is shown unchanged with a link to the page and its authors, under the CC BY-SA 4.0 licence. Photos must be public domain, CC0 or CC BY and must name an author. We skip CC BY-SA photos, flags, seals and logos. Photos load from Wikimedia and are not copied to this site. Satellite views are NASA GIBS true-colour images of the wider area from the day before, and may not show the event. A check in our build fails if any of these items lacks its credit.

How a score is made

Severity gives up to 50 points: the source's own level, or our reading of a magnitude or wind speed. Size gives up to 30. Freshness gives up to 20 and fades over about a day. Repeated US warnings of one kind lose 10 points each. The score only orders the list. It says nothing about danger to you.
